## Text

**5:1** For every high priest taken from among people is appointed on behalf of people in the things relating to God, so that he may offer gifts and sacrifices for sins,

**5:2** who is able to feel compassion for those who are ignorant and go astray, since he himself is also surrounded by weakness;

**5:3** and because of this, just as for the people, so also for himself he must make offerings for sins.

**5:4** Nor does anyone take the honor for himself, but rather he who is called by God, just as Aaron was.

**5:5** In this way Christ also did not glorify Himself to become a high priest, but He who spoke to Him: "You are My Son; today I have fathered You."

**5:6** Just as He also says in another place: "You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek."

**5:7** In the days of His flesh, offering prayers and supplications with a strong cry and tears to Him who was able to save Him from death, He was heard because of His reverence.

**5:8** And indeed, although He was the Son of God, He learned obedience from the things He suffered;

**5:9** and having been perfected, He became the cause of eternal salvation to all who obey Him,

**5:10** designated by God a high priest according to the order of Melchizedek.

**5:11** Concerning this we have a great discourse, and one that cannot be explained in words, because you have become weak in hearing.

**5:12** For indeed, although you ought to be teachers because of the time, you again need to be taught what the basic elements of the beginning of the words of God are, and you have become those who need milk, not solid food.

**5:13** For everyone who partakes of milk is inexperienced in the word of righteousness, for he is a small child.

**5:14** Solid food, however, belongs to the mature, to those who through habit have their senses trained for the discernment of good and evil.

## Translator Notes

- **5:2** — The Vulgate uses condolere ("to feel compassion for," literally "to suffer along with") to translate Greek metriopathein ("to deal gently with").
- **5:8** — The Vulgate explicitly reads Filius Dei ("the Son of God"), whereas the Greek text reads simply "a Son."
- **5:11** — Latin De quo may refer either to the subject matter ("Concerning this") or to Melchizedek ("Concerning him"). The Vulgate also renders Greek dysermeneutos ("hard to explain") with ininterpretabilis and nothroi ("dull") with imbecilles ("weak").
- **5:12** — The Vulgate uses the passive ut vos doceamini ("that you be taught"), compared with the Greek active construction ("that someone teach you").
