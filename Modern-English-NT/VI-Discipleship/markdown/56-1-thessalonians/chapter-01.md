## Text

**1:1** Paul, Silvanus, and Timothy, to the church of the Thessalonians in God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

**1:2** Grace to you and peace. We always give thanks to God for all of you, mentioning you in our prayers without interruption,

**1:3** mindful of the work of your faith, and of labor, and of love, and of the endurance of the hope of our Lord Jesus Christ before our God and Father,

**1:4** knowing, brothers loved by God, your election,

**1:5** because our gospel did not come to you in speech alone, but also in power, and in the Holy Spirit, and in much fullness, just as you know what sort of men we were among you for your sake.

**1:6** And you became imitators of us and of the Lord, receiving the word in much affliction, with joy of the Holy Spirit,

**1:7** so that you became a pattern to all who believe in Macedonia and in Achaia.

**1:8** For from you the word of the Lord has been spread widely, not only in Macedonia and in Achaia, but also in every place your faith, which is toward God, has gone out, so that it is not necessary for us to say anything.

**1:9** For they themselves report about us what kind of entrance we had to you, and how you turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God,

**1:10** and to wait for His Son from the heavens, whom He raised from the dead, Jesus, who has rescued us from the coming wrath.

## Translator Notes

- **1:2** — In the Clementine Vulgate, 'Grace to you and peace' (Gratia vobis, et pax) opens verse 2 rather than closing verse 1, and 'without interruption' (sine intermissione) closes verse 2 rather than beginning verse 3 as in modern Greek editions.
- **1:3** — The Clementine Vulgate reads 'et laboris, et caritatis' ('and of labor, and of love'), coordinating the two nouns with 'et' rather than rendering the Greek genitive phrase 'labor of love.'
- **1:5** — Vulgate 'in plenitudine multa' literally means 'in much fullness,' corresponding to the Greek phrase for 'in full conviction' or 'full assurance.'
- **1:10** — Vulgate 'eripuit' is perfect tense ('has rescued'), whereas the Greek text uses a present participle ('who rescues/delivers').
