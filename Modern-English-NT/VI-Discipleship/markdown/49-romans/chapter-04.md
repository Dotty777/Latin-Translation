## Text

**4:1** What then shall we say that Abraham, our father according to the flesh, has found?

**4:2** For if Abraham was justified by works, he has glory, but not before God.

**4:3** For what does Scripture say? "Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him for righteousness."

**4:4** Now to the one who works, the wage is not counted according to grace, but according to debt.

**4:5** But to the one who does not work, yet believes in Him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is reckoned for righteousness according to the purpose of the grace of God.

**4:6** Just as David also speaks of the blessedness of the person to whom God credits righteousness apart from works:

**4:7** "Blessed are those whose iniquities have been forgiven, and whose sins have been covered.

**4:8** Blessed is the man to whom the Lord has not counted sin."

**4:9** Does this blessedness, then, remain in circumcision only, or also in uncircumcision? For we say that faith was reckoned to Abraham for righteousness.

**4:10** How then was it reckoned? In circumcision, or in uncircumcision? Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision.

**4:11** And he received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith that is in uncircumcision, so that he might be the father of all who believe while in uncircumcision, so that it might be reckoned to them also for righteousness,

**4:12** and that he might be the father of circumcision, not only to those who are of circumcision, but also to those who follow the footsteps of the faith that is in the uncircumcision of our father Abraham.

**4:13** For the promise to Abraham or to his seed, that he would be heir of the world, was not through the law, but through the righteousness of faith.

**4:14** For if those who are of the law are heirs, faith has been made void; the promise has been abolished.

**4:15** For the law produces wrath. For where there is no law, neither is there transgression.

**4:16** Therefore it is by faith, so that according to grace the promise may be firm to all the seed, not only to the one who is of the law, but also to the one who is of the faith of Abraham, who is the father of us all

**4:17** (just as it is written: "I have appointed you a father of many nations") before God, whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls the things that are not as the things that are.

**4:18** Against hope, he believed in hope, so that he might become the father of many nations, according to what had been spoken to him: "So will your seed be."

**4:19** And he did not grow weak in faith, nor did he consider his own body dead, when he was already nearly a hundred years old, or the dead womb of Sarah.

**4:20** Moreover, at the promise of God he did not waver in distrust, but was strengthened in faith, giving glory to God,

**4:21** knowing most fully that whatever He had promised, He is also able to do.

**4:22** Therefore it was also reckoned to him for righteousness.

**4:23** Now it was not written for his sake alone that it was reckoned to him for righteousness,

**4:24** but also for our sake, to whom it will be reckoned as those who believe in Him who raised Jesus Christ our Lord from the dead,

**4:25** who was handed over for our offenses, and rose again for our justification.

## Translator Notes

- **4:5** — The concluding clause "secundum propositum gratiæ Dei" ("according to the purpose of the grace of God") is a distinctly Clementine Vulgate reading not present in the Greek text.
- **4:6** — The Vulgate renders the Greek accounting verb with the classical Latin bookkeeping idiom "accepto fert" (literally "enters as received," i.e., "credits").
- **4:8** — The Vulgate uses the perfect indicative "non imputavit" ("has not counted") rather than the emphatic future negation of the Greek ("will never count").
- **4:19** — The Vulgate includes the negation "nec consideravit" ("nor did he consider"), aligning with the Byzantine and Western textual tradition rather than critical Greek editions that omit the negative.
- **4:25** — The Vulgate uses the active verb "resurrexit" ("rose again") where the Greek has a passive verb ("was raised").
