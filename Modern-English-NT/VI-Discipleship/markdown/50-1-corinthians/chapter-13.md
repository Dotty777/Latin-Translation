## Text

**13:1** If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, yet do not have love, I have become like resounding bronze or a ringing cymbal.

**13:2** And if I have prophecy, and know all mysteries and all knowledge, and if I have all faith so as to move mountains, yet do not have love, I am nothing.

**13:3** And if I distribute all my possessions for the food of the poor, and if I hand over my body so that I may burn, yet do not have love, it benefits me nothing.

**13:4** Love is patient; it is kind. Love is not envious, does not act wrongly, is not puffed up,

**13:5** is not ambitious, does not seek what is its own, is not irritated, does not think evil,

**13:6** does not rejoice over injustice, but rejoices together with the truth;

**13:7** it bears everything, believes everything, hopes for everything, endures everything.

**13:8** Love never falls away, whether prophecies will be done away with, or tongues will cease, or knowledge will be destroyed.

**13:9** For in part we know, and in part we prophesy.

**13:10** But when what is perfect has come, what is in part will be done away with.

**13:11** When I was a little child, I spoke like a little child, I understood like a little child, I thought like a little child. Yet when I became a man, I did away with the things that belonged to a little child.

**13:12** We see now through a mirror in a riddle, but then face to face. Now I know in part, but then I will know just as I also have been known.

**13:13** But now there remain faith, hope, love, these three; yet the greatest of these is love.

## Translator Notes

- **13:3** — The Vulgate explicitly reads 'for the food of the poor' (in cibos pauperum) and 'so that I may burn' (ita ut ardeam), reflecting the Greek variant kauthesomai rather than kauchesomai ('so that I may boast').
- **13:4** — Latin non agit perperam literally means 'does not act wrongly' or 'perversely,' rendering Greek perpereuetai ('boasts') through the Latin adverb perperam.
- **13:5** — Latin non est ambitiosa ('is not ambitious') is a distinctly Vulgate rendering where the Greek reads ouk aschemonei ('does not act unbecomingly').
- **13:12** — Latin per speculum in aenigmate literally means 'through a mirror in a riddle,' evoking an indirect, obscure reflection as in an ancient metal mirror; the Vulgate also omits 'for' at the start of the verse.
