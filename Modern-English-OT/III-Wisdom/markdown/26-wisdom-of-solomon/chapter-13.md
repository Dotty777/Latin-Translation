## Text

**13:1** Now all people are foolish in whom the knowledge of God is not present; and from these good things that are seen, they could not understand Him who is, nor, paying attention to the works, did they recognize who the craftsman was;

**13:2** but either fire, or wind, or swift air, or the circle of the stars, or immense water, or the sun and the moon, they thought to be gods, rulers of the world.

**13:3** If, delighted by their beauty, they thought them to be gods, let them know how much more beautiful than these their sovereign is; for the originator of beauty established all these things.

**13:4** Or if they marveled at their power and works, let them understand from them that He who made these things is stronger than they;

**13:5** for from the greatness of beauty and of creation, the Creator of these things will be able to be recognizably seen.

**13:6** Yet still, concerning these there is less complaint; for even these perhaps go astray while seeking God and wishing to find Him.

**13:7** For indeed, while they go about among His works, they inquire, and they are persuaded that the things that are seen are good.

**13:8** Again, however, not even these ought to be excused.

**13:9** For if they were able to know so much that they could estimate the world, how did they not more easily find the Lord of it?

**13:10** But wretched are they, and their hope is among the dead, who called the works of human hands gods: gold and silver, the invention of craft, and likenesses of animals, or a useless stone, the work of an ancient hand.

**13:11** Or if some skilled carpenter cuts a straight piece of wood from the forest, and skillfully scrapes off all its bark, and, using his craft, diligently fashions a vessel useful for the conduct of life;

**13:12** and uses up the remnants of that work for the preparation of food,

**13:13** and the remnant of these that serves no use, a crooked piece of wood and full of knots, he carves diligently in his leisure, and through the knowledge of his craft shapes it, and likens it to the image of a human being,

**13:14** or compares it to one of the animals, coating it with red ocher, and making its color ruddy with dye, and coating over every blemish that is in it;

**13:15** and makes for it a worthy dwelling, both placing it on the wall and securing it with iron

**13:16** so that it may not happen to fall, watching out for it, knowing that it cannot help itself; for it is an image, and it has need of help.

**13:17** And concerning his possessions, and his children, and marriage, making a vow he inquires; he is not ashamed to speak with one who is without life.

**13:18** And indeed for health he pleads with one who is weak, and for life he begs one who is dead, and for help he calls upon one who is useless.

**13:19** And for a journey he asks of one who cannot walk; and concerning acquiring, and working, and the outcome of all things, he asks of one who is useless in all things.

## Translator Notes

- **13:2** — The Vulgate explicitly names "the sun and the moon" (solem et lunam) and "immense water" (nimiam aquam), where the Greek Septuagint has "luminaries of heaven" and "violent water."
- **13:5** — The Vulgate "recognizably" (cognoscibiliter, lit. "in a knowable way") and "of beauty and of creation" (speciei et creaturae) correspond to the Greek Septuagint "by analogy" and "greatness and beauty of created things."
- **13:11** — The Vulgate reads "a straight piece of wood" (lignum rectum), contrasting with "crooked" in verse 13, whereas the Greek Septuagint has "an easily handled piece of wood."
- **13:19** — The Vulgate "the outcome of all things" (omnium rerum eventu) and "useless in all things" (in omnibus est inutilis) generalize the Greek Septuagint, which refers specifically to "success of hands" and "most feeble with its hands."
