## Text

**10:1** Dying flies ruin the sweetness of ointment. Wisdom and glory are more precious than a small and temporary folly.

**10:2** A wise man's heart is at his right hand, and a fool's heart is at his left hand.

**10:3** And even while walking along the road, a fool, though he himself is unwise, considers all people to be fools.

**10:4** If the spirit of one who holds authority rises against you, do not abandon your place, because healing will cause the greatest sins to cease.

**10:5** There is an evil that I have seen under the sun, going out as if by error from the presence of a ruler:

**10:6** a fool placed in lofty dignity, and the rich sitting below.

**10:7** I have seen servants on horses, and princes walking on the earth like servants.

**10:8** Whoever digs a pit will fall into it, and whoever tears down a hedge, a snake will bite him.

**10:9** Whoever moves stones will be injured by them, and whoever splits wood will be wounded by it.

**10:10** If the iron is blunted, and it is not as before, but has become dull, it will be sharpened with much labor, and after diligence wisdom will follow.

**10:11** If a serpent bites in silence, the one who slanders secretly is in no way less than it.

**10:12** The words of a wise man's mouth are grace, and the lips of an unwise man will hurl him down;

**10:13** the beginning of his words is folly, and the end of his mouth is the worst error.

**10:14** A fool multiplies words. A man does not know what was before him; and what will be after him, who will be able to tell him?

**10:15** The labor of fools will afflict them, those who do not know how to go to the city.

**10:16** Woe to you, land, whose king is a boy, and whose princes eat in the morning.

**10:17** Blessed is the land whose king is noble, and whose princes dine at their proper time, for refreshment and not for indulgence.

**10:18** Through laziness the rafters will be brought low, and through weakness of hands the house will leak.

**10:19** For laughter they make bread and wine so that the living may feast; and everything obeys money.

**10:20** In your thought do not slander the king, and in the privacy of your bedroom do not curse a rich man, because even the birds of the sky will carry your voice, and what has wings will report the utterance.

## Translator Notes

- **10:1** — Latin muscae morientes literally means "dying flies" (unlike Hebrew "dead flies"). In the second sentence sapientia et gloria is taken as the subject and parva et ad tempus stultitia as the ablative of comparison; the Hebrew has the reverse sense ("a little folly outweighs wisdom and honor").
- **10:3** — The Vulgate resolves the ambiguous Hebrew clause as omnes stultos aestimat ("considers all people to be fools"), whereas the Hebrew can also mean that the fool reveals to everyone that he himself is a fool.
- **10:10** — The Vulgate uniquely uses passive exacuetur ("will be sharpened") and closes with post industriam sequetur sapientia ("after diligence wisdom will follow"), differing from the Hebrew Masoretic Text ("he must exert more strength, and wisdom has the advantage of giving success").
- **10:11** — The Vulgate reads in silentio ("in silence") and interprets the Hebrew "master of the tongue" (snake charmer) as qui occulte detrahit ("the one who slanders secretly"), who is nihil eo minus ("in no way less than it," i.e., the serpent).
- **10:19** — The Vulgate joins panem et vinum ("bread and wine") together as objects of faciunt ("they make") with the purpose clause ut epulentur viventes ("so that the living may feast"), and ends with pecuniae obediunt omnia ("everything obeys money," whereas the Hebrew says money "answers" everything).
