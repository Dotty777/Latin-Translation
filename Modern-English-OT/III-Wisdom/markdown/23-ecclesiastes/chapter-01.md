## Text

**1:1** The words of Ecclesiastes, son of David, king of Jerusalem.

**1:2** "Futility of futilities," said Ecclesiastes, "futility of futilities, and all things are futility."

**1:3** What more does a man have from all his labor at which he labors under the sun?

**1:4** A generation passes away, and a generation arrives, yet the earth stands forever.

**1:5** The sun rises and sets, and returns to its place; and rising anew there,

**1:6** it circles through the south and turns toward the north. Traversing all things in a circuit, the wind proceeds and returns into its own circles.

**1:7** All rivers enter the sea, and the sea does not overflow; to the place from which the rivers go out, they return so that they may flow again.

**1:8** All things are difficult; a man cannot explain them in speech. The eye is not sated with sight, nor is the ear filled with hearing.

**1:9** What is it that has been? The very thing that is to be. What is it that has been done? The very thing that is to be done.

**1:10** Nothing under the sun is new, nor is anyone able to say, "Look, this is fresh"; for it has already gone before in the ages that were before us.

**1:11** There is no remembrance of former things, nor indeed will there be recollection of those things that are to be afterward among those who are to be at the last.

**1:12** I, Ecclesiastes, was king of Israel in Jerusalem,

**1:13** and I resolved in my mind to seek and investigate wisely concerning all things that are done under the sun. This worst occupation God gave to the sons of men, so that they might be occupied in it.

**1:14** I saw all things that are done under the sun, and look, all things are futility and affliction of spirit.

**1:15** Perverse people are corrected with difficulty, and the number of fools is infinite.

**1:16** I spoke in my heart, saying, "Look, I have become great, and I have surpassed in wisdom all who were before me in Jerusalem; and my mind has contemplated many things wisely, and I have learned."

**1:17** And I gave my heart so that I might know prudence and learning, and errors and folly; and I recognized that in these too there was labor and affliction of spirit,

**1:18** because in much wisdom there is much indignation, and the one who adds knowledge adds labor as well.

## Translator Notes

- **1:1** — The Vulgate retains the Greek-derived title Ecclesiastes ("convener of an assembly" or "speaker before an assembly"), corresponding to the Hebrew Qohelet.
- **1:2** — Latin vanitas ("emptiness, futility, vanity") is used in the superlative genitive idiom vanitas vanitatum ("futility of futilities", traditionally "vanity of vanities") to express utter hollowness and transience.
- **1:5** — Unlike the Hebrew, where circling south and turning north describe the wind in verse 6, the Vulgate links "rising anew there, it circles through the south and turns toward the north" to the sun; in verse 6, Latin spiritus means "wind" (or "spirit").
- **1:14** — Latin afflictio spiritus ("affliction of spirit") is a distinctive Vulgate rendering of the Hebrew expression re'ut ruach ("striving after wind").
- **1:15** — Distinctively in the Vulgate, this proverb speaks of perverse people being corrected with difficulty and an infinite number of fools, whereas the Hebrew reads "what is crooked cannot be straightened, and what is lacking cannot be counted."
