## Text

**6:1** There is also another evil that I saw under the sun, and indeed common among people:

**6:2** a man to whom God has given riches, and possessions, and honor, and nothing is lacking to his soul of all that he desires, yet God has not granted him the power to eat from it, but a stranger will devour it: this is futility and a great misery.

**6:3** If someone fathers a hundred children, and lives many years, and has numerous days of age, and his soul does not enjoy the good things of his possessions, and he lacks a burial: concerning this one, therefore, I declare that a stillborn child is better than he is.

**6:4** For it comes in vain and goes to darkness, and its name will be erased by oblivion.

**6:5** It has not seen the sun, nor has it known the difference between good and evil.

**6:6** Even if he lives two thousand years and has not fully enjoyed good things, do not all things hasten to one place?

**6:7** All a person's labor is in his mouth, yet his soul will not be filled.

**6:8** What more does the wise person have than the fool? And what does the poor person have, except to go there where life is?

**6:9** It is better to see what you desire than to long for what you do not know. Yet this too is futility and presumption of spirit.

**6:10** As for the one who is yet to be, his name has already been called; and it is known that he is a human being, and cannot contend in judgment against one stronger than himself.

**6:11** There are very many words, and they have much futility in disputing.

**6:12** Why is it necessary for a person to seek things greater than himself, since he does not know what is beneficial for him in his life, for the number of the days of his sojourn and the time that passes by like a shadow? Or who will be able to show him what will be after him under the sun?

## Translator Notes

- **6:5** — The Vulgate reads neque cognovit distantiam boni et mali ("nor has it known the difference between good and evil"), whereas the Hebrew text compares the stillborn child's rest with that of the rich person.
- **6:8** — The Vulgate renders the second clause as nisi ut pergat illuc ubi est vita ("except to go there where life is"), differing from the Hebrew question about a poor person knowing how to walk before the living.
- **6:9** — The Vulgate reads videre quod cupias, quam desiderare quod nescias ("to see what you desire than to long for what you do not know") and translates the refrain as praesumptio spiritus ("presumption of spirit").
- **6:12** — The Vulgate opens with Quid necesse est homini majora se quaerere ("Why is it necessary for a person to seek things greater than himself"), expanding on the brief Hebrew question, and renders the days of vanity as peregrinationis suae ("of his sojourn").
