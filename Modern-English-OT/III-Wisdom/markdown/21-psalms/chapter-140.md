## Text

**140:1** A psalm of David. Lord, I have cried out to You; hear me. Give attention to my voice when I cry out to You.

**140:2** May my prayer be directed like incense in Your sight; the raising of my hands as an evening sacrifice.

**140:3** Place a guard, Lord, over my mouth, and a door of enclosure over my lips.

**140:4** Do not turn my heart aside to words of malice, to offer excuses in sins with people who practice iniquity; and I will not have fellowship with their chosen ones.

**140:5** A righteous person will correct me in mercy and will rebuke me; but may a sinner's oil not fatten my head. For my prayer also is still in their good pleasures:

**140:6** their judges, joined to the rock, have been swallowed up. They will hear my words, because they have prevailed.

**140:7** Just as the thickness of the earth has been broken open upon the earth, our bones have been scattered beside the underworld.

**140:8** For toward You, Lord, Lord, are my eyes; I have hoped in You; do not take away my soul.

**140:9** Guard me from the snare that they have set for me, and from the stumbling blocks of those who practice iniquity.

**140:10** Sinners will fall into his net; I myself am alone until I pass by.

## Translator Notes

- **140:3** — Latin 'ostium circumstantiae' ("a door of enclosure") follows the Septuagint ('perioches'), whereas the Hebrew Masoretic Text reads "keep watch over the door of my lips."
- **140:4** — Latin 'verba malitiae' ("words of malice") and 'ad excusandas excusationes' ("to offer excuses") follow the Septuagint; 'electis eorum' can mean either "their chosen ones" or "their choice things" (the Hebrew reads "delicacies").
- **140:5** — Latin 'oleum peccatoris' ("a sinner's oil") and 'in beneplacitis eorum' ("in their good pleasures") follow the Septuagint, whereas the Hebrew reads "oil upon the head" and "against their evil deeds."
- **140:6** — Latin 'juncti petrae' ("joined to the rock") renders Greek 'echomena petras' ("close beside the rock"), and 'quoniam potuerunt' ("because they have prevailed") reflects a Greek variant meaning "were strong/able" rather than "were pleasant/sweet."
- **140:10** — Latin 'retiaculo ejus' ("his net") is grammatically ambiguous, referring either to the sinner's own net or to an adversary's.
