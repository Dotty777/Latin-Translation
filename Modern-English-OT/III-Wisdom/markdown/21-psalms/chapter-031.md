## Text

**31:1** For David himself, of understanding. Blessed are those whose iniquities have been forgiven, and whose sins have been covered.

**31:2** Blessed is the man to whom the Lord has not imputed sin, nor is there deceit in his spirit.

**31:3** Because I remained silent, my bones grew old while I was crying out all day.

**31:4** Because day and night Your hand weighed heavily upon me, I was turned in my misery while a thorn is driven in.

**31:5** I made my offense known to You, and I did not hide my unrighteousness. I said, "I will confess my unrighteousness against myself to the Lord," and You forgave the ungodliness of my sin.

**31:6** For this every holy one will pray to You at an opportune time. Nevertheless, in a flood of many waters, they will not come near him.

**31:7** You are my refuge from the affliction that has surrounded me; my exultation, rescue me from those who surround me.

**31:8** I will give you understanding and instruct you in this way along which you will walk; I will set My eyes firmly upon you.

**31:9** Do not become like a horse and a mule, which have no understanding. With muzzle and bridle restrain the jaws of those who do not come near to You.

**31:10** Many are the scourges of the sinner, but mercy will surround the one who hopes in the Lord.

**31:11** Be glad in the Lord and exult, you righteous, and glory, all you who are upright in heart.

## Translator Notes

- **31:1** — The Vulgate superscription Ipsi David intellectus reflects the Septuagint ("For David, of understanding"), corresponding to the Hebrew term maskil (a contemplative or instructive psalm); Vulgate Psalm 31 corresponds to Psalm 32 in Hebrew numbering.
- **31:4** — Following the Septuagint, the Vulgate reads conversus sum in aerumna mea, dum configitur spina ("I was turned in my misery while a thorn is driven in"), whereas the Hebrew reads "my vitality was turned into the droughts of summer."
- **31:5** — Following the Septuagint, the Vulgate reads adversum me injustitiam meam ("my unrighteousness against myself"), where the Hebrew has "my transgressions."
- **31:9** — The Vulgate renders the second sentence with the imperative constringe ... ad te, traditionally read as a prayer to God to curb stubborn people who refuse to come near to Him, whereas the Hebrew describes curbing a horse or mule so it stays near its handler.
