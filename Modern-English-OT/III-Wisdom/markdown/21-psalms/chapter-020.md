## Text

**20:1** To the end. A psalm of David.

**20:2** Lord, in Your strength the king will rejoice, and over Your salvation he will exult exceedingly.

**20:3** You granted him the desire of his heart, and You did not deprive him of the wish of his lips.

**20:4** For You went before him with blessings of sweetness; You set on his head a crown of precious stone.

**20:5** He asked life from You, and You granted him length of days, forever, and forever and ever.

**20:6** Great is his glory in Your salvation; glory and great splendor You will place upon him.

**20:7** For You will give him as a blessing forever and ever; You will gladden him in joy with Your face.

**20:8** For the king hopes in the Lord, and in the mercy of the Most High he will not be shaken.

**20:9** May Your hand be found by all Your enemies; may Your right hand find all who hate You.

**20:10** You will make them like a fiery oven in the time of Your face: the Lord in His anger will confound them, and fire will devour them.

**20:11** You will destroy their fruit from the earth, and their seed from the sons of men,

**20:12** because they turned evils against You; they devised plans that they could not establish.

**20:13** For You will make them turn their backs; in Your remnants You will make ready their faces.

**20:14** Be exalted, Lord, in Your strength; we will sing and chant Your mighty deeds.

## Translator Notes

- **20:4** — Latin "dulcedinis" ("of sweetness") and "de lapide pretioso" ("of precious stone") follow the Septuagint, whereas the Hebrew reads "of good things" and "of fine gold."
- **20:9** — Latin passive "Inveniatur" ("May ... be found") follows the Septuagint where the Hebrew is active; while vv. 9-13 continue the address to the Lord in the Vulgate tradition, some interpreters take the second-person pronouns here as addressing the king.
- **20:10** — Latin "in tempore vultus tui" literally means "in the time of Your face" (meaning Your appearing or presence), and "conturbabit" ("will confound") follows the Septuagint where the Hebrew reads "will swallow up."
- **20:13** — Latin "pones eos dorsum" (literally "You will make them a back") is an idiom for driving enemies into flight; "in reliquiis tuis" ("in Your remnants") follows the Septuagint ("perileimmasin"), whereas the Hebrew reads "with your bowstrings."
