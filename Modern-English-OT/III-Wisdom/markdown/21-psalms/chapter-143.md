## Text

**143:1** A psalm of David. Against Goliath. Blessed be the Lord my God, who teaches my hands for battle and my fingers for war.

**143:2** My mercy and my refuge; my upholder and my deliverer; my protector, and in Him I have hoped, who subdues my people under me.

**143:3** Lord, what is man, that You have become known to him? Or the son of man, that You take account of him?

**143:4** Man has become like emptiness; his days pass by like a shadow.

**143:5** Lord, bend down Your heavens and come down; touch the mountains, and they will smoke.

**143:6** Flash lightning, and You will scatter them; send out Your arrows, and You will throw them into confusion.

**143:7** Send out Your hand from on high; rescue me and set me free from many waters, from the hand of the sons of foreigners,

**143:8** whose mouth has spoken emptiness, and their right hand is a right hand of iniquity.

**143:9** God, I will sing a new song to You; on a ten-stringed harp I will sing psalms to You.

**143:10** You who give salvation to kings, who redeemed Your servant David from the evil sword,

**143:11** rescue me and deliver me from the hand of the sons of foreigners, whose mouth has spoken emptiness, and their right hand is a right hand of iniquity;

**143:12** whose sons are like young plantings in their youth; their daughters are gracefully arrayed, adorned all around after the likeness of a temple.

**143:13** Their storehouses are full, overflowing from this into that; their sheep are prolific, abounding in their goings out;

**143:14** their cattle are fat. There is no collapse of a wall, nor passage through, nor outcry in their streets.

**143:15** They have called the people blessed to whom these things belong; blessed is the people whose God is the Lord.

## Translator Notes

- **143:1** — The superscription "Against Goliath" and the reading "my God" (where the Hebrew has "my rock") follow the Septuagint and are distinctive to the Vulgate tradition.
- **143:3** — Vulgate "innotuisti ei" ("You have become known to him," following the Septuagint) differs from the Hebrew Masoretic Text, which reads "that you know/regard him."
- **143:12** — Following the Septuagint, the Vulgate renders vv. 12-14 in the third person ("whose sons... their daughters... their storehouses"), continuing the description of the "sons of foreigners" from v. 11, whereas the Hebrew reads first-person plural ("our sons").
- **143:13** — Vulgate "ex hoc in illud" ("from this into that," mirroring the Septuagint) reflects a different reading of the Hebrew expression usually rendered "of every kind" (literally "from kind to kind").
- **143:15** — Vulgate "Beatum dixerunt" ("They have called ... blessed," following the Septuagint) includes a verb absent from the Hebrew, contrasting popular praise of material prosperity (vv. 12-14) with true blessedness in the Lord.
