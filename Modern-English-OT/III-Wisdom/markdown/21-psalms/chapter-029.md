## Text

**29:1** A psalm of song, at the dedication of the house of David.

**29:2** I will exalt You, Lord, because You have lifted me up, and You have not delighted my enemies over me.

**29:3** Lord my God, I cried out to You, and You healed me.

**29:4** Lord, You brought up my soul from the underworld; You saved me from those going down into the pit.

**29:5** Sing psalms to the Lord, His holy ones, and give thanks to the remembrance of His holiness.

**29:6** For anger is in His indignation, and life is in His will; at evening weeping will linger, and at morning, gladness.

**29:7** But I said in my abundance, "I will never be moved."

**29:8** Lord, in Your will You granted strength to my beauty; You turned Your face away from me, and I became troubled.

**29:9** To You, Lord, I will cry out, and to my God I will plead.

**29:10** What benefit is there in my blood, while I go down into corruption? Will dust give thanks to You, or declare Your truth?

**29:11** The Lord heard and had mercy on me; the Lord became my helper.

**29:12** You have turned my mourning into joy for me; You have torn apart my sackcloth and surrounded me with gladness,

**29:13** so that my glory may sing to You, and I may not feel compunction. Lord my God, I will give thanks to You forever.

## Translator Notes

- **29:4** — Latin infernus ("the underworld") denotes the abode of the dead. Also, a descendentibus in lacum ("from those going down into the pit") follows the Septuagint and the Hebrew consonantal text rather than "from going down into the pit."
- **29:6** — Vulgate ira in indignatione ejus ("anger is in His indignation") follows the Septuagint, whereas the Hebrew reads "His anger is for a moment."
- **29:8** — Vulgate decori meo ("to my beauty") follows the Septuagint, contrasting with the Hebrew "to my mountain."
- **29:11** — Following the Septuagint, the Vulgate uses past-tense statements ("The Lord heard... became my helper") where the Hebrew has imperatives ("Hear... be my helper").
- **29:13** — Vulgate non compungar ("I may not feel compunction," from compungo, to pierce with sorrow or remorse) follows the Septuagint, whereas the Hebrew reads "and not be silent."
