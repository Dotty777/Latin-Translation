## Text

**27:1** Because of poverty many have transgressed, and the one who seeks to become rich turns his eye away.

**27:2** Just as a peg is driven into the middle of a joint of stones, so also right between selling and buying sin will be squeezed in.

**27:3** If you do not hold yourself steadfastly in the fear of the Lord, your house will quickly be overthrown.

**27:4** Just as dust will remain in the shaking of a sieve, so will a man's perplexity in his thought.

**27:5** The furnace tests the vessels of the potter, and the testing of tribulation tests righteous people.

**27:6** Just as the cultivation of a tree shows its fruit, so too a word from the thought of a man's heart.

**27:7** Before speech, do not praise a man, for this is the testing of people.

**27:8** If you pursue righteousness, you will lay hold of her and put her on like a full-length robe of honor, and you will dwell with her, and she will protect you forever, and on the day of recognition you will find a firm support.

**27:9** Birds gather to those like themselves, and truth will return to those who practice it.

**27:10** A lion always lies in wait for prey; so do sins for those who practice iniquities.

**27:11** A holy man remains in wisdom like the sun, whereas a fool changes like the moon.

**27:12** In the midst of the senseless, reserve speech for the right time, but in the midst of the thoughtful, be constantly present.

**27:13** The talk of sinners is hateful, and their laughter is in the delights of sin.

**27:14** Speech that swears much will make the hair of the head stand on end, and its irreverence is a stopping of the ears.

**27:15** The shedding of blood is in the quarreling of the proud, and their cursing is grievous to hear.

**27:16** Whoever lays bare a friend's secrets loses trust, and he will not find a friend after his own mind.

**27:17** Love your neighbor, and unite yourself with him in trust.

**27:18** For just as a man who loses his friend, so also is the one who loses the friendship of his neighbor.

**27:19** And just as one who releases a bird from his hand, so you have abandoned your neighbor, and you will not catch him.

**27:20** Do not pursue him, because he is far away; for he has escaped like a gazelle from a snare, because his soul has been wounded.

**27:21** You will no longer be able to bind him up. And for a curse there is reconciliation.

**27:22** The one who winks with the eye devises wicked things, and no one will cast him off.

**27:23** In the sight of your eyes he will sweeten his mouth, and he will marvel at your speeches; but in the end he will twist his mouth, and in your words he will place a stumbling block.

**27:24** I have hated many things, and I have not counted them equal to him; and the Lord will hate him.

**27:25** Whoever throws a stone high up, it will fall upon his head; and a treacherous blow of the treacherous man will deal out wounds.

**27:26** And whoever digs a pit will fall into it, and whoever places a stone for his neighbor will stumble over it, and whoever sets a snare for another will perish in it.

**27:27** As for the one who carries out a most wicked plan, it will roll down upon him, and he will not recognize from where it comes to him.

**27:28** Mockery and reproach belong to the proud, and vengeance like a lion will lie in wait for him.

**27:29** Those who delight in the fall of the righteous will perish by a snare, and anguish will consume them before they die.

**27:30** Anger and fury, both are detestable, and a sinful man will hold fast to them.

## Translator Notes

- **27:4** — The Vulgate reads pulvis ("dust") and the Greek loanword aporia ("perplexity" or "distress"), whereas the Greek Septuagint reads "refuse" and "rubbish."
- **27:8** — Latin poderem denotes a foot-length priestly robe. The entire second half of the verse ("and you will dwell with her... find a firm support") is a Vulgate expansion from the Old Latin tradition absent from the shorter Greek text.
- **27:11** — The Vulgate reads Homo sanctus in sapientia manet sicut sol ("A holy man remains in wisdom like the sun"), whereas the Greek Septuagint reads "The discourse of a godly person is always wisdom."
- **27:18** — The Vulgate reads amicum suum ("his friend") and third-person qui perdit ("the one who loses"), whereas the Greek Septuagint reads "his enemy" (or "a dead man") and addresses the reader in the second person.
- **27:21** — Following the added clause at the end of v. 20, the Vulgate negates the opening clause ("you will no longer be able to bind him up," vs. Greek "a wound can be bound up") and omits the closing clause about one who reveals secrets losing hope.
