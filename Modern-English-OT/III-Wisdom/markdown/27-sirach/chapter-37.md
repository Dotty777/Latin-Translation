## Text

**37:1** Every friend will say, "I also have joined in friendship," but there is a friend who is a friend in name alone. Is not sorrow present even to death?

**37:2** Yet a companion and a friend will turn to enmity.

**37:3** Most wicked presumption, from where were you created to cover the dry land with wickedness and its deceitfulness?

**37:4** A companion rejoices together with a friend in pleasures, and in the time of distress he will be an adversary.

**37:5** A companion suffers together with a friend for the sake of the belly, and against an enemy he will take up a shield.

**37:6** Do not forget your friend in your mind, and do not be unmindful of him in your wealth.

**37:7** Every counselor puts forth counsel, but there is a counselor for himself.

**37:8** Guard your soul from a counselor; know first what his need is, for he himself will think in his own mind,

**37:9** so that he may not thrust a stake into the ground and say to you:

**37:10** Do not take counsel with one who lies in wait for you, and hide counsel from those who are jealous of you.

**37:11** Confer with an irreligious man about holiness, and with an unjust man about justice, and with a woman about her who is a rival, with a timid man about war, with a merchant about transport, with a buyer about a sale, with an envious man about giving thanks,

**37:12** but be continually with a holy man, whomever you know to be observing the fear of God.

**37:13** Establish a heart of good counsel with yourself, for there is nothing else of greater value to you than it.

**37:14** The soul of a holy man at times declares the truth more than seven watchmen sitting on high to keep watch.

**37:15** And in all these things implore the Most High, that He may direct your way in truth.

**37:16** Before all works let a truthful word go before you, and before every action, steady counsel.

**37:17** A wicked word will change the heart,

**37:18** from which four parts arise: good and evil, life and death; and their continual ruler is the tongue. There is a shrewd man who is an instructor of many, and he is useless to his own soul.

**37:20** He who speaks with sophistry is hateful; in every matter he will be defrauded.

**37:21** Grace has not been given to him by the Lord, for he has been defrauded of all wisdom.

**37:22** There is a wise man who is wise for his own soul, and the fruit of his understanding is praiseworthy.

**37:23** A wise man instructs his people, and the fruits of his understanding are trustworthy.

**37:24** A wise man will be filled with blessings, and those who see him will praise him.

**37:25** The life of a man is in a number of days, but the days of Israel are innumerable.

**37:26** A wise man among the people will inherit honor, and his name will be living forever.

**37:27** Son, in your life test your soul, and if it is wicked, do not give it power.

**37:28** For not all things are beneficial for all, and not every kind pleases every soul.

**37:29** Do not be greedy at every feast, and do not pour yourself out over every food.

**37:30** For in many foods there will be sickness, and greediness will draw near even to biliousness.

**37:31** Because of overindulgence many have died, but he who is temperate will add to his life.

## Translator Notes

- **37:3** — Latin "praesumptio" ("presumption") corresponds to "evil inclination/thought" in the Greek, and the Vulgate adds "malitia et ... illius" ("with wickedness and its deceitfulness") to the clause about covering the dry land.
- **37:9** — The Clementine Vulgate reads "mittat sudem in terram" ("thrust a stake into the ground," where the Greek has "cast a lot") and breaks off at "et dicat tibi:" without the counselor's quoted words found in the Greek.
- **37:11** — Unlike the Greek ("do not consult"), the Clementine Vulgate lacks a negative before the imperative "tracta" ("confer"), giving the series an ironic force leading into the contrast of v. 12, and omits several additional examples present in the Greek.
- **37:17** — The Vulgate reads "Verbum nequam immutabit cor" ("A wicked word will change the heart"), whereas the Greek speaks of a trace of the heart's change.
