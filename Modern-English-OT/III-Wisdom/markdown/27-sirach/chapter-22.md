## Text

**22:1** With a muddy stone a lazy person has been stoned, and everyone will speak about the scorn for him.

**22:2** With ox dung a lazy person has been stoned, and everyone who touches him will shake off his hands.

**22:3** A father's shame is over an undisciplined son, and a daughter will turn out to be a loss.

**22:4** A prudent daughter is an inheritance to her husband, whereas one who brings shame becomes a disgrace to the one who fathered her.

**22:5** A brazen woman brings shame upon father and husband, and she will not be less than the ungodly; and by both she will be dishonored.

**22:6** Music in mourning is untimely discourse; lashes and instruction are wisdom at all times.

**22:7** Whoever teaches a fool is like one who glues together a potsherd.

**22:8** Whoever speaks a word to one who is not listening is like one who rouses a sleeper from deep sleep.

**22:9** One who recounts wisdom to a fool speaks with a sleeper, and at the end of the discourse he says, "Who is this?"

**22:10** Weep over a dead person, for his light has failed; and weep over a fool, for understanding has failed.

**22:11** Weep a little over a dead man, because he has found rest;

**22:12** for the most wicked life of a most wicked man is worse than the death of a fool.

**22:13** Mourning for a dead person is seven days, but for a fool and an ungodly person it is all the days of their life.

**22:14** Do not speak much with a fool, and do not go with a senseless person.

**22:15** Guard yourself from him, so that you may not have trouble, and you will not be defiled by his sin.

**22:16** Turn aside from him, and you will find rest, and you will not grow weary in his folly.

**22:17** What will be heavier than lead? And what other name is there for it than fool?

**22:18** Sand and salt and a mass of iron are easier to bear than a person who is imprudent and foolish and ungodly.

**22:19** A wooden tie-beam bound into the foundation of a building will not come apart; so also is a heart established in the thought of counsel.

**22:20** The thought of a sensible person will at no time be corrupted by fear.

**22:21** Just as stakes on high places, and rough stones laid without mortar, will not remain against the face of the wind.

**22:22** So also a timid heart in the thought of a fool will not withstand the onslaught of fear.

**22:23** Just as a trembling heart in the thought of a fool will at no time fear, so also the one who remains in the precepts of God always.

**22:24** One who pricks an eye draws forth tears, and whoever pricks a heart brings forth feeling.

**22:25** One who throws a stone at birds will bring them down; so also one who reviles a friend dissolves friendship.

**22:26** Against a friend, even if you have drawn a sword, do not despair, for there is a return. Against a friend,

**22:27** if you have opened a bitter mouth, do not be afraid, for there is reconciliation, except for reviling, and reproach, and pride, and the revealing of a secret, and a treacherous blow; in all these things a friend will flee.

**22:28** Keep faith with a friend in his poverty, so that you may also rejoice in his good things.

**22:29** In the time of his affliction remain faithful to him, so that you may also be a co-heir in his inheritance.

**22:30** Before the fire of a furnace vapor and smoke of fire rise high; so also before bloodshed are curses, and insults, and threats.

**22:31** I will not be ashamed to greet a friend; I will not hide myself from his face, and if evils happen to me through him, I will endure.

**22:32** Everyone who hears will guard himself against him.

**22:33** Who will place a guard over my mouth, and a sure seal upon my lips, so that I may not fall because of them and my tongue may not destroy me?

## Translator Notes

- **22:1** — Vulgate reads lapidatus est ("has been stoned") here and in 22:2, whereas the Greek reads "is compared to."
- **22:5** — The clause et ab impiis non minorabitur is a Vulgate-distinct addition absent from the standard Greek; it can mean either "she will not be less [wicked] than the ungodly" or "she will not be belittled by the ungodly."
- **22:11** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:11 (continuing 22:10 = Saints 22:11).
- **22:12** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:12 (Vulgate-distinct comparative using 'super', "more than / worse than", continuing 22:11).
- **22:15** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:15 (parallel to Saints/Greek 22:13b).
- **22:16** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:16 (continuing 22:15 = Saints 22:13c); Latin 'acediaberis' renders Greek 'akediaseis' ("be weighed down / grow weary").
- **22:17** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:17 (parallel to Saints.Bible/Greek 22:14), replacing the prior empty-alignment placeholder.
- **22:20** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:20 (parallel to Saints/Greek 22:16b).
- **22:21** — Latin sine impensa uses impensa in its architectural sense of "mortar" or "binding material." The line completing the comparison (about a timid heart) now stands in restored native Vulgate 22:22.
- **22:22** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:22 (completing the comparison of 22:21 = Saints 22:18; previously claimed absent in the note on old 22:18).
- **22:23** — Restored native Clementine Vulgate verse 22:23 (Vulgate-distinct doublet following 22:22, reading 'cor trepidum ... non metuet' as it stands in Clem1598-USFX).
- **22:26** — In the Clementine Vulgate verse division, the opening phrase Ad amicum ("Against a friend") of the second sentence stands at the end of native 22:26 (Saints 22:21) and continues into native 22:27 (Saints 22:22), where Vulgate also adds triste ("bitter") to "mouth."
- **22:31** — Vulgate reads salutare ("to greet") and adds sustinebo ("I will endure"), whereas the Greek reads "to shelter" and connects "if harm happens to me through him" directly to native 22:32 (Saints 22:26).
