## Text

**29:1** He who practices mercy lends to his neighbor, and he who prevails with his hand keeps the commandments.

**29:2** Lend to your neighbor in the time of his need, and in turn repay your neighbor in his due time.

**29:3** Confirm your word and deal faithfully with him, and at every time you will find what is necessary for you.

**29:4** Many have regarded a loan as a find, and have brought trouble to those who helped them.

**29:5** Until they receive it, they kiss the hands of the giver, and in their promises they humble their voice.

**29:6** But if he is able to repay, he will resist; he will scarcely repay half of the full sum, and he will count it as a find.

**29:7** Many have not lent, not out of wickedness, but they feared being defrauded for nothing.

**29:8** Nevertheless, toward the humble man be stronger in spirit, and do not keep him waiting for alms.

**29:9** Because of the commandment, receive the poor man, and because of his poverty do not send him away empty.

**29:10** Lose money for the sake of your brother and your friend, and do not hide it under a stone to be lost.

**29:11** Place your treasure in the precepts of the Most High, and it will profit you more than gold.

**29:12** Store up alms in the heart of the poor, and this will intercede for you from all evil.

**29:13** More than the shield of a mighty man and more than a spear, it will fight against your enemy.

**29:14** A good man gives a pledge for his neighbor, and he who has lost shame will abandon him to himself.

**29:15** Do not forget the kindness of a guarantor, for he has given his life for you.

**29:16** A sinner ascribes the goods of a guarantor to himself, and one ungrateful in mind will abandon the one delivering him.

**29:17** A most wicked guarantee has ruined many who were prospering, and has tossed them about like the waves of the sea.

**29:18** Whirling mighty men about, it has caused them to migrate, and they have wandered among foreign nations.

**29:19** A sinner transgressing the commandment of the Lord will fall into a wicked pledge, and he who attempts to do many things will fall into judgment.

**29:20** Rescue your neighbor according to your strength, and watch out for yourself so that you do not fall.

**29:21** The beginning of a man's life is water and bread, and clothing, and a house covering shame.

**29:22** Better is the sustenance of a poor man under a shelter of planks than splendid banquets abroad without a home.

**29:23** Let the least amount please you in place of much, and you will not hear the reproach of staying abroad.

**29:24** It is a wretched life, lodging from house to house; and where he lodges, he will not act confidently, nor will he open his mouth.

**29:25** He will lodge as a guest, and will feed and give drink to the ungrateful, and in addition to these things he will hear bitter words:

**29:26** "Come over, guest, and set the table, and with what you have in your hand, feed the others."

**29:27** "Depart from before the honor of my friends; because of the need of my house, my brother has become my guest."

**29:28** These things are burdensome to a man who has understanding: the rebuke of a house and the reproach of a lender.

## Translator Notes

- **29:6** — Vulgate "if he is able to repay, he will resist" makes the borrower the subject (whereas the Greek refers to the lender recovering half), and "solidi" denotes the full principal sum (or a solidus coin).
- **29:12** — Vulgate uniquely reads "in the heart of the poor" (in corde pauperis) and "will intercede for you" (pro te exorabit), where the Greek has "in your storehouses" and "will deliver you."
- **29:14** — Latin "confusionem" denotes a sense of shame; Vulgate "derelinquet sibi" ("will abandon him to himself") uses a reflexive dative where the Greek simply reads "will forsake him."
- **29:16** — Vulgate "ascribes to himself" (sibi ascribit) differs from the Greek, which reads "will overturn" (ruin) the guarantor's property.
- **29:27** — Vulgate "from before the honor of my friends" adds "of my friends" (amicorum meorum) to the Greek "from the face of honor," meaning to step aside out of respect for honored guests.
