## Text

**4:1** Now Simon, who was mentioned earlier, the informer concerning the money and his homeland, was speaking evil about Onias, as if he himself had provoked Heliodorus to these things and he himself had been the instigator of the evils,

**4:2** and he dared to call the provider for the city, the defender of his own nation, and the zealot for the law of God a plotter against the kingdom.

**4:3** But when the hostilities advanced to such a point that murders were even committed through certain close associates of Simon,

**4:4** Onias, considering the danger of the strife and that Apollonius, being commander of Coele-Syria and Phoenicia, was raging to increase the malice of Simon, went to the king,

**4:5** not as an accuser of the citizens, but considering within himself the common benefit of the entire multitude.

**4:6** For he saw that without royal oversight it was impossible for peace to be given to affairs, and that Simon could not cease from his folly.

**4:7** But after the departure of Seleucus from life, when Antiochus, who was called the Noble, had assumed the kingdom, Jason, the brother of Onias, sought the high priesthood,

**4:8** having approached the king, promising him three hundred sixty talents of silver, and from other revenues eighty talents.

**4:9** In addition to these, he also promised another one hundred fifty, if it were granted to his authority to establish for himself a gymnasium and a training school for youths, and to enroll those who were in Jerusalem as Antiochenes.

**4:10** When the king had consented to this and he had obtained the leadership, he immediately began to bring his fellow tribesmen over to the Gentile custom;

**4:11** and having removed those things which had been established for the Jews out of kindness by the kings through John, the father of Eupolemus, who carried out a lawful embassy before the Romans concerning friendship and alliance, abandoning the rights of the citizens, he ratified perverse practices.

**4:12** For indeed he dared to establish a gymnasium beneath the citadel itself, and to place all the finest of the youths in brothels.

**4:13** Now this was not a beginning, but a certain increase and advance of Gentile and foreign conduct, because of the abominable and unheard-of crime of the impious Jason, who was no priest,

**4:14** so that the priests were no longer devoted to the duties of the altar, but having despised the temple and neglected the sacrifices, they hurried to become participants in the wrestling arena and its unjust provision, and in the exercises of the discus.

**4:15** And indeed, holding ancestral honors as nothing, they judged Greek glories to be the best,

**4:16** for the sake of which a dangerous strife held them, and they emulated their practices, and desired in all things to be altogether like those whom they had held as enemies and destroyers.

**4:17** For to act impiously against the divine laws does not go unpunished; but the time that follows will make this clear.

**4:18** Now when the five-year contest was being celebrated at Tyre and the king was present,

**4:19** the wicked Jason sent sinful men from Jerusalem, carrying three hundred didrachmas of silver for the sacrifice of Hercules, which those who had conveyed them requested should not be spent on sacrifices, because it was not fitting, but that they be assigned to other expenses.

**4:20** Yet these were indeed offered for the sacrifice of Hercules by the one who had sent them; because of those who were present, however, they were given for the construction of trireme ships.

**4:21** Now after Apollonius, the son of Mnestheus, had been sent into Egypt on account of the leading men of King Ptolemy Philometor, when Antiochus had learned that he had become estranged from the affairs of the kingdom, looking out for his own interests, departing from there he came to Joppa, and from there to Jerusalem.

**4:22** And having been magnificently received by Jason and the city, he entered with the lights of torches and with praises; and from there he turned his army into Phoenicia.

**4:23** And after a period of three years, Jason sent Menelaus, the brother of the Simon mentioned above, carrying the money to the king and intending to deliver answers concerning necessary affairs.

**4:24** But he, having been commended to the king, when he had magnified the appearance of his authority, turned the high priesthood back onto himself, adding three hundred talents of silver above Jason.

**4:25** And having received orders from the king, he came, having indeed nothing worthy of the priesthood, but bearing the spirit of a cruel tyrant and the wrath of a wild beast.

**4:26** And Jason indeed, who had taken his own brother captive, being himself deceived, was driven out as a fugitive into the Ammonite region.

**4:27** Now Menelaus indeed obtained the leadership, but concerning the money promised to the king he did nothing, even while Sostratus, who was placed over the citadel, was demanding collection,

**4:28** for to this man belonged the collection of tributes; for which reason both of them were summoned to the king.

**4:29** And Menelaus was removed from the priesthood, with his brother Lysimachus succeeding him; Sostratus, however, was placed over the Cyprians.

**4:30** And while these things were taking place, it happened that the people of Tarsus and Mallus stirred up an insurrection, because they had been given as a gift to Antiochis, the king's concubine.

**4:31** Therefore the king came in haste to calm them, having left Andronicus, one of his companions, as deputy.

**4:32** Now Menelaus, supposing that he had received an opportune time, having stolen certain golden vessels from the temple, presented them to Andronicus, and he had sold others at Tyre and throughout the neighboring cities.

**4:33** When Onias had learned this with complete certainty, he rebuked him, while keeping himself in a safe place at Antioch beside Daphne.

**4:34** Accordingly, Menelaus, approaching Andronicus, asked that he kill Onias. When he had come to Onias, and after giving right hands with an oath (although he was suspected by him) had persuaded him to come out from the sanctuary, he immediately killed him, not fearing justice.

**4:35** For this reason not only the Jews, but also the other nations were indignant and were distressed over the unjust murder of so great a man.

**4:36** But when the king returned from the regions of Cilicia, the Jews at Antioch approached him, together with the Greeks as well, complaining about the wicked murder of Onias.

**4:37** Therefore Antiochus, saddened in spirit on account of Onias and moved to mercy, shed tears, remembering the sobriety and moderation of the deceased;

**4:38** and inflamed in spirit, he ordered Andronicus, stripped of his purple robe, to be led around through the entire city, and in the very same place in which he had committed the impiety against Onias, for the sacrilegious man to be deprived of life, with the Lord repaying him a fitting punishment.

**4:39** Now when many acts of sacrilege had been committed in the temple by Lysimachus at the counsel of Menelaus, and the report had spread abroad, a multitude gathered together against Lysimachus, much gold having already been carried out.

**4:40** Now as the crowds rose up and their spirits were filled with wrath, Lysimachus, having armed nearly three thousand men, began to use wicked hands, with a certain tyrant as leader, advanced equally in age and in madness.

**4:41** But when they perceived the attempt of Lysimachus, some seized stones, others stout clubs, and some threw ashes at Lysimachus.

**4:42** And indeed many were wounded, and some were even struck down, and all were turned to flight; even the sacrilegious man himself they killed beside the treasury.

**4:43** Concerning these things, therefore, a trial began to be conducted against Menelaus.

**4:44** And when the king had come to Tyre, three men sent by the elders brought the matter before him.

**4:45** And when Menelaus was being overcome, he promised to give Ptolemy much money to persuade the king.

**4:46** Therefore Ptolemy approached the king, who was in a certain courtyard as if for the sake of cooling off, and drew him away from his decision;

**4:47** and Menelaus indeed, guilty of every malice, he acquitted of the charges; but the wretched men who, even if they had pleaded their case before the Scythians, would have been judged innocent, these he condemned to death.

**4:48** Quickly, therefore, they paid an unjust penalty, who had pursued the case on behalf of the city, the people, and the sacred vessels.

**4:49** On account of this, the Tyrians also, being indignant, showed themselves most generous regarding their burial.

**4:50** Menelaus, however, on account of the greed of those who were in power, remained in authority, growing in malice for plots against the citizens.

## Translator Notes

- **4:7** — The Vulgate translates Antiochus IV's Greek epithet Epiphanes ("Illustrious") into Latin as Nobilis ("the Noble").
- **4:12** — The Vulgate reads in lupanaribus ("in brothels"), whereas the Greek Septuagint reads hypo petason ("under the petasos," the broad-brimmed Greek hat associated with the gymnasium).
- **4:19** — The Vulgate reads viros peccatores ("sinful men") carrying three hundred didrachmas of silver, whereas the Greek describes them as envoys (Antiochenes from Jerusalem) carrying three hundred drachmas.
- **4:29** — The Vulgate states that Menelaus was removed from the priesthood with Lysimachus succeeding him and that Sostratus was placed over the Cyprians, whereas the Greek has Menelaus leaving Lysimachus as deputy and Sostratus leaving Crates, commander of the Cyprians.
- **4:40** — The Vulgate reads duce quodam tyranno ("with a certain tyrant as leader"), whereas the Greek Septuagint has the proper name Auranus.
