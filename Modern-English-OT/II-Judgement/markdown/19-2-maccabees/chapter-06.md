## Text

**6:1** But not long afterward, the king sent a certain old man from Antioch to compel the Jews to depart from the ancestral laws and the laws of God,

**6:2** and also to defile the temple that was in Jerusalem and to name it after Olympian Jupiter, and the one on Gerizim, according to what those who inhabited the place were, after Hospitable Jupiter.

**6:3** Now the onslaught of evils was extremely severe and grievous to all:

**6:4** for the temple was full of debauchery and revelries of the Gentiles, and of men fornicating with prostitutes; and into the sacred buildings women thrust themselves of their own accord, bringing inside things that were not lawful.

**6:5** The altar also was full of unlawful things that were forbidden by the laws.

**6:6** Moreover, neither were the Sabbaths kept, nor were the ancestral festival days observed, nor did anyone simply confess himself to be a Jew.

**6:7** And they were led with bitter compulsion on the king's birthday to the sacrifices; and when the sacred rites of Liber were celebrated, they were forced, crowned with ivy, to walk in procession for Liber.

**6:8** Moreover, a decree went out to the neighboring cities of the Gentiles, at the suggestion of the people of Ptolemais, that they themselves should also act in the same way against the Jews, so that they would offer sacrifice,

**6:9** and should kill those who were unwilling to go over to the customs of the Gentiles. So misery was there to be seen.

**6:10** For two women were denounced for having circumcised their children; and after they had led the women around publicly through the city with the infants hung at their breasts, they hurled them headlong over the walls.

**6:11** Others, however, assembling in nearby caves and secretly celebrating the Sabbath day, when they had been reported to Philip, were burned with flames, because they feared, on account of religion and observance, to bring aid to themselves by hand.

**6:12** Now I implore those who are about to read this book not to recoil because of adverse events, but to consider that the things that happened were not for the destruction, but for the discipline of our race.

**6:13** For indeed, not to allow sinners for a long time to act as they please, but immediately to inflict punishments, is a sign of great kindness.

**6:14** For not as with other nations, where the Lord waits patiently so that when the day of judgment arrives He may punish them in the fullness of their sins,

**6:15** has He likewise determined in our case, that once our sins have come to their end, only then should He take vengeance on us.

**6:16** Therefore He indeed never removes His mercy from us; yet while disciplining in adversities, He has not abandoned His people.

**6:17** But let these things be said by us in few words as a reminder to the readers. For now we must come to the narrative.

**6:18** Therefore Eleazar, one of the foremost scribes, a man advanced in age and noble in countenance, with his mouth opened and gaping, was compelled to eat pork.

**6:19** But he, embracing a most glorious death rather than a loathsome life, went forward voluntarily to torture.

**6:20** And considering how he ought to come forward, enduring patiently, he resolved not to admit unlawful things out of love for life.

**6:21** But those who stood by, moved by unjust pity because of their longstanding friendship with the man, took him aside privately and urged that meats be brought that were lawful for him to eat, so that he might pretend to have eaten from the meats of the sacrifice, as the king had commanded,

**6:22** so that by doing this he might be delivered from death; and because of their old friendship with the man, they showed him this kindness.

**6:23** But he began to consider the worthy eminence of his age and old age, and the gray hair of his inborn nobility, and the deeds of his most excellent way of life from boyhood; and in accordance with the ordinances of the holy law established by God, he answered quickly, saying that he wished to be sent ahead into the underworld.

**6:24** "For it is not worthy of our age," he said, "to pretend, so that many of the young men, supposing that Eleazar at ninety years of age has gone over to the way of life of foreigners,

**6:25** should themselves be deceived because of my pretense and because of a short span of perishable life, and through this I should bring stain and detestation upon my old age.

**6:26** For even if at the present time I should be rescued from the tortures of men, yet neither alive nor dead will I escape the hand of the Almighty.

**6:27** Therefore, by departing from life courageously, I will indeed show myself worthy of old age,

**6:28** and I will leave a courageous example to the young men, if with a ready spirit and courageously I undergo an honorable death for the most solemn and most holy laws." When these things had been said, he was immediately dragged to torture.

**6:29** Now those who were leading him, and who a little while before had been gentler, were turned to anger because of the words spoken by him, which they supposed had been uttered out of arrogance.

**6:30** But as he was being put to death by blows, he groaned and said, "Lord, You who have holy knowledge, You clearly know that although I could be delivered from death, I endure severe pains of the body; yet in my soul, out of fear of You, I gladly suffer these things."

**6:31** And indeed this man departed from life in this way, leaving behind not only to the young men, but also to the entire nation, the memory of his death as an example of virtue and courage.

## Translator Notes

- **6:1** — The Vulgate reads senem quemdam Antiochenum ("a certain old man from Antioch"), whereas the Greek Septuagint refers to an Athenian elder or senator.
- **6:2** — Here and in 6:7, the Vulgate uses the Roman divine names Jupiter (Jovis) and Liber (Liberi) where the Greek Septuagint has Zeus and Dionysus.
- **6:14** — The clause cum judicii dies advenerit ("when the day of judgment arrives") is a Vulgate-distinct reading absent from the Greek Septuagint; the opening negative Non enim governs ita et in nobis statuit in 6:15.
- **6:20** — The Vulgate wording ("considering how he ought to come forward...") diverges notably from the Greek Septuagint, which states that Eleazar spat out the meat before advancing.
- **6:21** — The Vulgate reads iniqua miseratione commoti ("moved by unjust pity"), where the Greek Septuagint describes those in charge of the unlawful sacrificial meal.
