## Text

**9:1** At that same time, Antiochus was returning in disgrace from Persia.

**9:2** For he had entered the city that is called Persepolis, and attempted to plunder the temple and oppress the city; but as the multitude rushed together to arms, they were turned to flight, and so it happened that Antiochus returned shamefully after the flight.

**9:3** And when he had come near Ecbatana, he learned what had been done concerning Nicanor and Timothy.

**9:4** Carried away in anger, however, he supposed that he could turn back upon the Jews the wrong done by those who had put him to flight; and therefore he ordered his chariot to be driven on, making the journey without pause, while heavenly judgment was pressing hard upon him, because he had spoken so arrogantly that he would come to Jerusalem and make it a burial heap of the Jews.

**9:5** But the Lord God of Israel, who sees all things, struck him with an incurable and invisible blow. For as soon as he finished this very speech, a terrible pain of the bowels seized him, and bitter torments of his inner parts,

**9:6** and indeed quite justly, since he had tortured the bowels of others with many and new tortures, although he in no way ceased from his malice.

**9:7** Moreover, filled with pride beyond this, breathing fire in his spirit against the Jews, and commanding the matter to be hastened, it happened that as he rushed along at full speed he fell from the chariot, and his limbs were racked by a severe impact of his body.

**9:8** And he who imagined himself to command even the waves of the sea, filled with pride beyond human measure, and to weigh the heights of the mountains on a balance, now brought low to the ground, was being carried in a litter, bearing witness in himself to the manifest power of God;

**9:9** so that worms swarmed out from the body of the ungodly man, and while he lived in pains his flesh fell away, and the army too was weighed down by his smell and stench;

**9:10** and the man who a little while before thought that he could touch the stars of heaven, no one could carry him because of the unbearable stench.

**9:11** From this point, therefore, brought down from his heavy pride, he began to come to a recognition of himself, warned by the divine blow, as his pains increased at every single moment.

**9:12** And when not even he himself could bear his own stench any longer, he spoke thus: "It is right to be subject to God, and for a mortal not to think as if equal to God."

**9:13** Now this wicked man prayed to the Lord, from whom he was not going to obtain mercy.

**9:14** And the city toward which he was coming in haste to bring it down to the ground and make it a tomb of piled-up bodies, he now wishes to make free;

**9:15** and the Jews, whom he had said he would not consider worthy even of burial, but would hand over to be torn apart by birds and wild beasts and would wipe out along with their little children, he now promises that he will make equal to the Athenians;

**9:16** that he will also adorn with the finest gifts the holy temple, which he had previously plundered, and multiply the sacred vessels, and furnish from his own revenues the expenses pertaining to the sacrifices;

**9:17** and beyond these things, that he will also become a Jew, and travel through every place of the earth, and proclaim the power of God.

**9:18** But as his pains did not cease (for the just judgment of God had come upon him), giving up hope, he wrote to the Jews a letter in the manner of a supplication, containing these things:

**9:19** "To the most excellent Jewish citizens: warmest greeting, good health, and happiness, from Antiochus the king and the princes.

**9:20** If you are in good health, and your children also, and everything is according to your wish, we give the greatest thanks.

**9:21** And I, being in a state of illness, yet kindly mindful of you after returning from the regions of Persia, and having been seized by a severe illness, have considered it necessary to take care for the common welfare,

**9:22** not despairing of myself, but having great hope of escaping the illness.

**9:23** Bearing in mind, moreover, that my father also, at the times when he was leading an army in the upper regions, pointed out who would take up the rule after him,

**9:24** so that if anything adverse should happen, or anything difficult should be reported, those who were in the regions, knowing to whom the supreme charge of affairs had been left, would not be troubled;

**9:25** and in addition to this, considering how all the powerful men nearby and the neighbors are lying in wait for opportunities and awaiting the outcome, I have designated my son Antiochus as king, whom I often commended to many of you when returning to the upper kingdoms; and I have written to him what is appended below.

**9:26** Therefore I implore and ask you, mindful of the benefits shown publicly and privately, that each one preserve loyalty toward me and toward my son.

**9:27** For I am confident that he will act moderately and humanely, and following my purpose, will also be accommodating to you."

**9:28** So the murderer and blasphemer, most severely struck, just as he himself had treated others, ended his life by a miserable death in a foreign land in the mountains.

**9:29** Now Philip, his foster brother, was transporting the body; fearing the son of Antiochus, he departed into Egypt to Ptolemy Philometor.

## Translator Notes

- **9:19** — The Clementine Vulgate reads the plural principes ("princes" or "rulers") alongside Antiochus, whereas the Greek has the singular title strategos ("commander" or "general") referring to Antiochus himself.
- **9:20** — Unlike the Greek text, which adds "to God" (or "to heaven"), the Vulgate simply reads "we give the greatest thanks" (maximas agimus gratias).
- **9:26** — The Clementine Vulgate prints Ora (an orthographic variant for Oro, "I implore"), pairing in the first-person singular with et peto ("and ask") and taking the accusative vos ("you").
